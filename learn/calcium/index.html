Calcium

The bone and muscle mineral.

Calcium is the most abundant mineral in your body. Most of it forms the structure of your bones and teeth, and a small amount circulates in your blood to help muscles, nerves and blood vessels work.

What it does

  • Strong bones and teethProvides the structure that keeps bones and teeth hard.
  • Muscles and nervesNeeded for muscles to contract and nerves to carry messages.
  • Blood vessels and clottingHelps blood vessels tighten and relax, and helps blood clot.

Low calcium intake can affect bones without obvious symptoms.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • Women after menopause, when bone loss speeds up
  • People who avoid dairy, for example because of lactose intolerance, a milk allergy or a vegan diet
  • People who get little vitamin D, which is needed to absorb calcium

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Over years: weaker bones (osteoporosis) and a higher risk of fractures
  • In children: rickets; in adults: osteomalacia (more often from low vitamin D)
  • Very low blood calcium can cause tingling, numbness and muscle cramps

Low calcium intake usually causes no obvious symptoms until bones weaken. Blood calcium is tightly controlled and stays normal even when intake is low.

Too much

The upper limit from all sources is 2,500 mg a day for adults 19–50 and 2,000 mg a day for adults 51 and over. Too much from supplements may increase kidney-stone risk and can cause constipation. These limits are not targets.

Medicines that can interact

  • LevothyroxineCalcium can reduce absorption of this thyroid medicine, so doses are usually spaced apart.
  • Quinolone antibioticsCalcium can reduce how much of these antibiotics your body absorbs.
  • DolutegravirCalcium supplements can lower the blood levels of this HIV medicine unless timed with food.
  • LithiumLithium can raise blood calcium, so calcium supplements need a clinician’s check.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

Blood calcium does not show whether you eat enough, because the body keeps it in a narrow range. A clinician may use bone-density scans and other tests to assess bone health.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
