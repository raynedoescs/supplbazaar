Iodine

The thyroid mineral.

Iodine is a trace element found in some foods and added to iodised salt. Your thyroid uses it to make the hormones that regulate metabolism.

Daily reference150 mcg
Upper limit1,100 mcg
Top food sourcesIodised salt, dairy, seafood

What it does

  • Thyroid hormonesAn essential part of the thyroid hormones T4 and T3.
  • MetabolismThyroid hormones control many of the body’s reactions and its rate of metabolism.
  • Growth and brain developmentNeeded for normal growth, bone development and brain development, especially in pregnancy and infancy.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Everyone · 19 and over150 mcg a day

Needs are higher in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Ask your clinician.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Common in Indian kitchens

  • Iodised salt (instead of regular salt)
  • Milk and dahi
  • Paneer
  • Eggs
  • Fish

India’s salt iodisation programme makes iodised salt the main source for most people. Rock salt (sendha namak) and many speciality salts are not iodised.

Choose iodised salt instead of regular salt, not more salt: WHO advises under 5 g of salt a day.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • People who do not use iodised salt
  • Pregnant women, who need more
  • Vegans and people who eat few or no dairy products, seafood or eggs
  • People living where soils are low in iodine, often mountainous areas
  • People with low iodine who eat a lot of soy, cassava or raw cabbage-family vegetables

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Goitre: a swelling at the front of the neck
  • Signs of an underactive thyroid, such as tiredness, feeling cold and dry skin
  • In pregnancy and infancy, harm to the baby’s growth and brain development

Thyroid problems have many causes, and too much iodine can cause the same signs as too little. A clinician checks thyroid function before anything else.

Too much

The US adult upper limit is 1,100 mcg a day from all sources. Too much iodine can cause the same thyroid problems as too little, including goitre and an underactive or overactive thyroid. This limit is not a target.

Medicines that can interact

  • Antithyroid medicinesTaking iodine with medicines for an overactive thyroid, such as methimazole, can push thyroid levels too low.
  • ACE inhibitors and potassium-sparing diureticsPotassium iodide supplements with these blood-pressure medicines can raise blood potassium.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

Urine iodine shows recent intake across a population rather than one person’s status. For individuals, clinicians usually check thyroid function (TSH).

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
