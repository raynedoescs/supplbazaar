Vitamin C

The collagen and healing vitamin.

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin. Unlike most animals, humans cannot make it, so it has to come from food or supplements.

What it does

  • Collagen and healingNeeded to make collagen, the protein that holds skin, blood vessels and other tissues together, and for wounds to heal.
  • Absorbing plant ironHelps your body absorb iron from plant foods.
  • AntioxidantHelps protect cells from damage and supports the immune system.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Men · 19 and over90 mg a day
Women · 19 and over75 mg a day

People who smoke need 35 mg a day more. Needs are higher in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Amla and guava are rich, everyday sources. Amounts vary with ripeness and storage.

Eat fruit and vegetables fresh where you can; cooking and storage reduce vitamin C.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • People who smoke, and people around second-hand smoke
  • People who eat a limited variety of foods
  • People with absorption problems or some chronic illnesses, including kidney dialysis and some cancers

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Tiredness and feeling run down
  • Easy bruising and tiny red or purple spots on the skin
  • Slow wound healing
  • Joint pain
  • Rough, bumpy skin and corkscrew-shaped body hairs

Severe deficiency (scurvy) is rare and can appear within a month of eating less than about 10 mg a day. Milder shortfalls are more common.

Too much

The US adult upper limit is 2,000 mg a day from all sources. Vitamin C has low toxicity, but large doses can cause diarrhoea, nausea and stomach cramps. This limit is not a target.

Medicines that can interact

  • Chemotherapy and radiationAntioxidant supplements might affect these treatments. Anyone having cancer treatment should ask their oncologist before taking vitamin C.
  • Statins with niacinAntioxidant supplements including vitamin C may reduce the benefit of some cholesterol treatment combinations. Ask your doctor.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

A blood test for vitamin C mostly reflects recent intake. A clinician interprets it with your diet and symptoms.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
