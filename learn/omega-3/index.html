Essentials

Omega-3

The essential fats.

Omega-3s are polyunsaturated fats. ALA, found in plant foods, is the only omega-3 your body cannot make at all; EPA and DHA, found mostly in fish, can be made from ALA but only in small amounts.

Daily reference (ALA)1.1–1.6 g
Upper limitNone set (see note)
Top food sourcesOily fish, flaxseed, walnuts
Most at riskLittle fish, very low-fat diets

What it does

  • Every cellPart of the membranes that surround every cell in the body.
  • Eyes and brainDHA is especially concentrated in the eyes and brain.
  • Heart, blood vessels and immunityUsed to make signalling molecules that affect the heart, blood vessels, lungs and immune system.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Men · 19 and over1.6 g ALA a day
Women · 19 and over1.1 g ALA a day

These Adequate Intakes are for ALA only. No intake target is set for EPA and DHA, and needs are higher in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Vegetarian diets get ALA from seeds, nuts and oils but very little EPA or DHA.

Plant foods give ALA; oily fish gives EPA and DHA.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • People who eat little or no oily fish
  • People who eat only plant foods, who get ALA but almost no EPA or DHA
  • People on very low-fat diets
  • People with conditions that limit fat absorption

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Rough, scaly skin
  • Itchy, inflamed skin (dermatitis)
  • In practice, true deficiency is almost unheard of in healthy people

Classical omega-3 deficiency is virtually nonexistent in healthy people. Most concern is about low intake, not a deficiency disease.

Too much

No US upper limit is set. High doses of EPA and DHA (2 g a day or more) might increase bleeding time, which matters with blood thinners such as warfarin; Europe’s food-safety authority considers up to about 5 g a day of EPA and DHA from supplements safe long-term.

Medicines that can interact

  • Warfarin and other blood thinnersOmega-3 supplements might add to the effect of blood thinners. Ask your doctor before taking them together.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

There is no routine test. Blood omega-3 levels can be measured, but there are no agreed cut-offs that show a problem.

Omega-3 levels can be measured, but there are no agreed cut-offs. A clinician looks at your diet, health and medicines together.
