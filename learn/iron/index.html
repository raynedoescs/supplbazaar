Iron

The oxygen-carrying mineral.

Iron is a mineral that helps your blood carry oxygen around your body. Food has two kinds: haem iron in meat and fish, which absorbs well, and non-haem iron in plants, which absorbs less well.

What it does

  • Carries oxygenA key part of haemoglobin, the red blood cell protein that moves oxygen from your lungs to the rest of your body.
  • Supports musclesPart of myoglobin, which supplies oxygen to muscles.
  • Growth and brain developmentNeeded for growth, brain development and many cell functions.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Men · 19–50 years8 mg a day
Women · 19–50 years18 mg a day
Everyone · 51 and over8 mg a day

Iron needs are higher during pregnancy, and a vegetarian diet may need more because plant iron is absorbed less well. Ask your clinician.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • Women with heavy periods
  • Frequent blood donors
  • People with stomach or bowel conditions, or who have had gut surgery
  • People with cancer or heart failure
  • Pregnant women, infants and young children, who have higher needs
  • People who eat little or no meat, because plant iron is absorbed less well

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Tiredness and weakness
  • Difficulty concentrating
  • Feeling unusually cold
  • Shortness of breath or a racing heart on exertion
  • Upset stomach
  • Sometimes cravings for ice or non-food items

Tiredness has many causes. If iron is truly low, a clinician also needs to find out why, such as blood loss or poor absorption.

Too much

The upper limit for adults is 45 mg a day from all sources. Too much iron can cause stomach upset and, at high doses, serious harm. Keep iron supplements away from children. Do not start iron based on a quiz result.

Medicines that can interact

  • LevodopaIron can reduce absorption of this Parkinson’s medicine.
  • LevothyroxineIron can reduce absorption of this thyroid medicine, so doses are usually spaced apart.
  • Proton pump inhibitorsAcid-reducing medicines can reduce iron absorption.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

Blood tests such as haemoglobin and ferritin (a marker of stored iron). A clinician decides which tests are useful and looks for the cause of low iron.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
