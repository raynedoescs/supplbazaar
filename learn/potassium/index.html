Electrolytes

Potassium

The fluid-balance electrolyte.

Potassium is an electrolyte, a mineral that carries an electrical charge. It is found in all body tissues, mostly inside your cells, and is plentiful in fruit, vegetables and pulses.

Daily reference2,600–3,400 mg
Upper limitNone set (see note)
Top food sourcesPulses, potatoes, fruit

What it does

  • Fluid balanceWorks with sodium to keep the right amount of fluid inside and outside your cells.
  • Nerves and musclesNeeded for nerves to send signals and for muscles, including the heart, to contract.
  • Blood pressureEnough potassium alongside less sodium supports healthy blood pressure.

How much you need

Eating a variety of fruit, vegetables and pulses is the usual route to enough.

A low dietary intake is different from a low blood potassium level. Ask a clinician before supplementing.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • People with inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s or ulcerative colitis)
  • People who use diuretics (water tablets) or laxatives
  • People with ongoing vomiting or diarrhoea
  • People with pica (craving non-food items such as clay)

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Mild: constipation, tiredness, muscle weakness and feeling unwell
  • More serious low potassium can affect the heart’s rhythm
  • Seek urgent care for palpitations or severe weakness

Low blood potassium is rare in healthy people with normal kidneys, and usually comes from medicines or fluid losses.

Too much

No upper limit has been set for healthy adults because the kidneys remove extra potassium. But that does not mean unlimited amounts are safe: with kidney disease or some medicines, high potassium can be dangerous. Potassium supplements and salt substitutes are not suitable for everyone.

Medicines that can interact

  • ACE inhibitors and ARBsThese blood-pressure and heart medicines can raise blood potassium.
  • Potassium-sparing diureticsThese water tablets can raise blood potassium.
  • Loop and thiazide diureticsThese water tablets increase potassium loss and can lower it.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

A blood test for potassium. Below about 3.6 mmol/L is considered low (hypokalaemia). A clinician interprets it with your medicines and kidney function.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
