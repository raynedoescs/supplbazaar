Electrolytes

Sodium

The salt mineral. Most of us get too much.

Sodium is an electrolyte found in table salt and in many processed foods. Your body needs some, but almost every population eats far more than it needs, and the usual health problem is too much, not too little.

What it does

  • Fluid and blood volumeHelps maintain the volume of fluid in your blood and around your cells.
  • NervesHelps nerves transmit signals.
  • Acid-base balance and cellsHelps keep the body’s chemistry balanced so cells work normally.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Adultsless than 2,000 mg sodium a day (less than 5 g salt)

This is a limit to stay below, not a minimum or a target. Do not add salt or take salt tablets because of a check-in score.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Where sodium hides

  • Bread and bakery items
  • Processed meat
  • Snack foods
  • Sauces and condiments such as soy and fish sauce
  • Packaged soups and instant noodles
  • Pickles
  • Cheese

Common in Indian kitchens

  • Achaar (pickles)
  • Papad
  • Namkeen and packaged snacks
  • Instant noodles
  • Packaged masala mixes

Foods that naturally contain sodium, such as milk, meat and shellfish, contribute less than processed foods. These are things to be mindful of, not foods to increase.

Low blood sodium is a water-balance issue, not simply a sign of eating too little salt.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • Almost everyone, for eating too much (the global average is more than double the WHO limit)
  • For low blood sodium: people who sweat heavily for long periods while drinking large amounts of plain water
  • People with ongoing vomiting or diarrhoea
  • People taking diuretics (water tablets)
  • People with heart failure, kidney disease or cirrhosis

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Confusion, irritability and restlessness
  • Headache and fatigue
  • Loss of appetite, nausea and vomiting
  • Muscle weakness, spasms or cramps
  • In severe cases: convulsions

Low blood sodium is mainly a water-balance problem, not a sign of eating too little salt. Sodium deficiency is extremely unlikely in healthy people. Severe or sudden symptoms need urgent medical care.

Too much

Too much sodium raises blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease, stroke and kidney disease. Most people already get enough, often too much. Do not add salt or take salt tablets because of this score.

Medicines that can interact

  • Diuretics (water tablets)These increase sodium lost in urine and can lower blood sodium.
  • Salt substitutesMany contain potassium. They are not suitable for everyone, particularly with kidney problems or some blood-pressure medicines.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

A blood sodium test. Low blood sodium is diagnosed by a clinician, who also looks for the cause.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
