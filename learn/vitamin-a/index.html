Vitamin A

The vision vitamin.

Vitamin A is a group of fat-soluble compounds. Preformed vitamin A (retinol) comes from animal foods and supplements; beta-carotene and other carotenoids in plants are turned into vitamin A as the body needs it.

What it does

  • EyesightAn essential part of the pigment in the eye that lets you see in low light.
  • Immune systemSupports immune function.
  • Growth and organsSupports cell growth and the normal formation of the heart, lungs, eyes and other organs.

How much you need

WhoDaily reference
Men · 19 and over900 mcg RAE a day
Women · 19 and over700 mcg RAE a day

Needs change in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and too much preformed vitamin A in pregnancy can harm the baby. Ask your clinician.

General healthy-adult daily reference, not a supplement dose. Needs vary by age and life stage. Upper limits are not intake targets.

Who is more at risk, and signs of low levels

Who is more at risk

  • People with limited access to animal foods and orange or green produce
  • People with cystic fibrosis
  • People with coeliac disease, Crohn’s disease or pancreas conditions that affect fat absorption
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women in low-income settings, and young children

Signs that can go with low levels

  • Night blindness: difficulty seeing in low light (usually the first sign)
  • Very dry eyes, which can damage the cornea if untreated
  • More frequent infections

Deficiency is rare where diets are varied, but it remains a leading cause of preventable blindness in children worldwide.

Too much

The US adult upper limit is 3,000 mcg a day of preformed vitamin A (retinol); beta-carotene from plants does not count. Too much preformed vitamin A can damage the liver and cause birth defects, so anyone who is or could become pregnant should not take vitamin A supplements without medical advice. This limit is not a target.

Medicines that can interact

  • Retinoid medicinesAcne and psoriasis medicines such as isotretinoin are related to vitamin A; taking vitamin A supplements with them can cause toxicity.

Never stop or change a medicine because of what you read here. Ask your doctor or pharmacist.

How it is measured

Blood retinol. Levels stay normal until liver stores run very low, so a clinician interprets the result with diet and symptoms.

A clinician can decide which tests are useful and interpret them with your symptoms and diet. Blood tests do not always reflect the body's nutrient stores.
